## Card 1: Always an implicit "instead of"

**Core knowledge**: Raising the question of whether to work on what interests you most always implies a trade-off against something else — usually what pays best.

**Explanation**: If all other things were equal, there would be no reason not to work on what interests you the most; so merely raising the question implies the other things are not equal. And if your main goal is to make money, you can't usually afford to work on what interests you the most, because people pay you for doing what they want.

**Q**: According to the article, why does merely raising the question show that all other things are not equal?

**Source anchor**: "People pay you for doing what they want, not what you want."

## Card 2: The exception: when you both want the same thing

**Core knowledge**: There is an obvious exception to the money trade-off — when you and whoever is paying you want the same thing, you can be paid well for what interests you.

**Explanation**: The article's example is football: if you love it and you're good enough at it, you can get paid a lot to play it. The article also says the odds are against you in a case like that, because so many other people like playing it too — and that this is not to say you shouldn't try, since it depends how much ability you have and how hard you're willing to work.

**Q**: According to the article, what is the obvious exception to the trade-off?

**Source anchor**: "But there's an obvious exception: when you both want the same thing."

## Card 3: Strange tastes raise the odds

**Core knowledge**: The odds of being paid for what interests you are better when you have strange tastes — when you like something that pays well and that few other people like.

**Explanation**: The article's example is Bill Gates, who truly loved running a software company. He didn't just love programming, which a lot of people do; he loved writing software for customers. The article calls that "a very strange taste indeed," and notes that if you have it, you can make a lot by indulging it.

**Q**: According to the article, what made Bill Gates's taste "a very strange taste indeed"?

**Source anchor**: "when you like something that pays well and that few other people like"

**Core knowledge**: Some people have a genuine intellectual interest in making money, and it is distinct from mere greed.

**Explanation**: The article describes them as unable to help noticing when something is mispriced, and unable to help doing something about it — it is like a puzzle for them.

**Q**: According to the article, how does a genuine intellectual interest in making money differ from mere greed?

**Source anchor**: "This is distinct from mere greed."
